How a video becomes one number: Δz

What Δz is

Every chart below is built from one quantity: for a given base world and a given physical factor (say, gravity), we render two clips that are identical in every way — same start position, same start velocity, same random seed — except that one constant (gravity) is set to a different value. We run both clips through the frozen V-JEPA encoder to get two vectors, then subtract:

Δz = z(intervention clip) − z(baseline clip)

Δz is a list of 32,768 numbers describing exactly how the model's internal representation moved, purely because of that one changed physical constant and nothing else.

How each z (32,768 numbers) is built
  1. Take 64 frames evenly spaced across the 6-second clip (the model's required input length).
  2. Run them through the frozen encoder. Its output is 8,192 "tokens" × 1,024 numbers each.
  3. Those 8,192 tokens are really 32 time-steps × 256 spatial patches (16×16 grid over the 256×256 frame), each a 1,024-number vector — confirmed from the model's own patch-embedding code, which lays tokens out in that time-major order.
  4. Average the 256 spatial patches together at each of the 32 time-steps, leaving 32 vectors of 1,024 numbers.
  5. Lay those 32 vectors end to end: 32 × 1,024 = 32,768 numbers. That is z.

This matches the design doc's rule: keep the model's own time resolution instead of averaging the whole clip into one blurred snapshot.

Uncentered SVD spectrum

What it measures

For one factor (e.g. gravity), stack every Δz we have for it into a table: one row per world/intervention pair, 32,768 columns. Singular value decomposition (SVD) finds a set of directions, ranked by how much of the rows' total squared length ("energy") each one accounts for.

How the chart is computed
cumulative energy at rank r = (sum of top-r singular values²) / (sum of all singular values²)

r90 = the smallest r where that fraction reaches 90%. A truly compact code would keep r90 small and flat no matter how many worlds you add.

What the result actually shows here

r90 ranges 4 (friction) to 28 (velocity) out of 50 Δz pairs per factor — friction's spectrum is genuinely compact (90% of its energy in just 4 directions), while velocity and gravity need most of their available rank. With 50 pairs per factor (versus 9–10 in the earlier 5-world pilot), r90 is no longer automatically pinned near the sample count, so this is a first real read on compactness rather than a sample-size artifact — though the docx's confirmatory design (hundreds of pairs) is still what would make these numbers precise.

Specificity matrix

What it measures

Fit a rank-8 basis using only one factor's Δz vectors (the row). Project a different factor's Δz vectors (the column) onto that basis and measure what fraction of their energy it recovers:

retention = 1 − ‖residual‖² / ‖original‖²

If gravity's own basis explains gravity's changes much better than friction's basis does, that's evidence gravity has its own distinct direction rather than all factors just producing generic "the ball moved differently" motion.

A fix worth knowing about

An earlier version of this matrix fit and tested the diagonal on the same data, which trivially made every diagonal cell look better than the off-diagonal cells regardless of any real signal. The diagonal here uses the same held-out (leave-one-world-out) retention as the chart below, so every cell — diagonal included — is tested on data its basis never saw.

What the result actually shows here

Gravity, restitution, and velocity are each specific: each factor's own diagonal cell is the largest value in its row (gravity 0.638, restitution 0.678, velocity 0.391), meaning each factor's basis reconstructs its own held-out effect far better than any other factor's basis does. Gravity and restitution show real cross-talk with each other (gravity's basis retains 0.299 of restitution's effect, and vice versa 0.293) — physically sensible, since both act on the sphere's vertical dynamics. Friction shows no specificity: its own diagonal (0.037) is the smallest value in its row — velocity's basis (0.062) explains friction's held-out variation better than friction's own basis does. Combined with friction's very low mean-shift energy fraction (2%) and tiny effect norm below, this suggests V-JEPA's embedding barely registers this friction range as a distinct signal at all, rather than registering it as a signal that gets confused with other factors.

Leave-one-world-out rank retention

What it measures

Whether a direction learned from some worlds actually predicts the direction of change in a new world it never saw — the generalization test every number above depends on.

How it's computed
  1. Hold out one world's Δz vectors for this factor.
  2. Fit a rank-8 uncentered SVD basis on the remaining worlds' Δz vectors only.
  3. Project the held-out world's Δz onto that basis and measure retained energy (same formula as the specificity matrix).
  4. Repeat once per world (25 folds here), average the 25 retention values.
A number to calibrate against

A random rank-8 direction in a 32,768-dimensional space would be expected to explain about rank / dimension = 8 / 32,768 ≈ 0.00024 of a held-out vector's energy purely by chance (the math memo's H₀-geometry null). Every bar below (0.037–0.678) sits far above that chance floor. Gravity, restitution, and velocity all clear 0.39–0.68 — a strong, structured signal, not noise. Friction sits at 0.037: still ~150× the chance floor, so the model is not completely blind to friction, but two orders of magnitude weaker than the other three factors and (per the specificity matrix) not even the best-explaining basis for its own effect.

Per-factor summary

Mean-shift energy fraction, explained

What share of a factor's average Δz-squared-length is explained just by one single "always shift this way" vector (the mean of all its Δz), versus needing a different direction per world:

fraction = ‖mean(Δz)‖² / mean(‖Δz‖²)

Low across the board (2–21%) — every factor's effect direction depends noticeably on the specific world rather than being one constant shift added to every embedding. Restitution is highest (21%): changing bounciness produces the most world-independent, consistent embedding shift of the four.
